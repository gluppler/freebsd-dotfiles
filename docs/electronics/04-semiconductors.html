Diodes and transistors

The PN junction

Pure silicon barely conducts. Doping it with a few phosphorus atoms leaves free electrons (N-type); boron leaves holes, missing electrons that move like positive charges (P-type). Where the two meet, carriers cross and cancel, leaving a thin depletion region with no free carriers and a built-in barrier of about 0.7 V.

Diodes also protect: in series against a reversed battery, across a relay coil against flyback, and from a pin to each rail against static discharge.

Rectifiers

A bridge of four diodes turns AC into DC by steering both halves of the cycle through the load the same way.

A capacitor across the load fills at each peak and supplies the load between peaks, leaving a ripple that falls as the capacitor grows. For a full-wave rectifier at mains frequency f, with load current I:

ΔV≈I2fC

Bipolar transistors

In an NPN transistor a small current into the base lets a current β times larger, typically 100 to 300, flow from collector to emitter. The base-emitter junction is a diode, so it sits near 0.65 V when on.

iC=βiBiE=iC+iBvBE≈0.65V

As a switch, drive it into saturation with more base current than IC/β needs; aim for a forced gain of 10 to 20.

As an amplifier the collector current depends exponentially on vBE. Its transconductance, the change of collector current per volt at the base, sets the gain of a common-emitter stage with collector resistor RC:

gm=ICVTAv≈−gmRC

MOSFETs

A MOSFET's gate is insulated: it draws no steady current, only a burst to charge its capacitance. Above the threshold Vth the gate voltage opens a channel from drain to source. With a little drain-source voltage the channel acts as a resistor (the triode region); with more, the current saturates:

iD=k[(vGS−Vth)vDS−12vDS2]vDS<vGS−Vth
iD=k2(vGS−Vth)2vDS≥vGS−Vth

Fully on, a MOSFET dissipates I2RDS(on). Pick a logic-level part whose RDS(on) is specified at your gate voltage, 2.5 or 4.5 V; its threshold is only where it begins to turn on.

P=I2RDS(on)

Every power MOSFET has a body diode from source to drain, drawn or not, so it blocks only one direction.

The CMOS inverter

Logic chips are built from complementary pairs: a P-channel transistor to the supply and an N-channel to ground, with their gates joined. One is always off, so no current flows except while switching.

Each switch charges the load capacitance C of the wiring and the next gates, so a CMOS chip's power grows with its clock frequency and the square of its supply voltage, the reason chips run at low voltages:

P=αCVDD2fα: the fraction of nodes switching each cycle

The H-bridge

Four switches around a motor let current go through it either way, so it can turn both ways. In practice the switches are MOSFETs, often in one driver chip with the protection built in.
