From circuit to board

A printed circuit board, PCB, holds the parts and joins them with copper. Designing one is a sequence of steps, each checked before the next; a PCB design program such as KiCad, which is free, handles all of them.

The schematic is the source of truth: the board is checked against it. Keep it readable, with inputs on the left, outputs on the right and named nets instead of long wires (Schematic symbols).

Layers and stack-up

A board is a stack of insulating layers, usually the glass-fibre epoxy called FR-4, with copper between them. A two-layer board has copper on both faces of one core. A four-layer board adds two inner layers, normally used as solid ground and power planes, and is the easiest way to make a fast or radio circuit work.

So 1 A needs about 0.30 mm on an outer layer and 2 A about 0.78 mm. Signal tracks carry microamps to milliamps: 0.15 to 0.25 mm is usual. Use wide tracks or filled areas of copper, pours, for power.

A track 0.25 mm wide and 100 mm long is 400 squares: 197 mΩ, which drops 0.20 V at 1 A. Copper's resistance also rises by 0.4% for each degree.

Return currents and ground planes

Every current returns to its source. On a board with a ground plane, the return current of each signal flows in the plane, and where it flows depends on frequency:

The rules that follow: keep one unbroken ground plane under all signals, on a four-layer board the layer next to the signals. Do not split it into separate analog and digital grounds unless a datasheet insists. Where a signal changes layers, put a ground via beside its via so the return current can change layers too.

Placing decoupling capacitors

A decoupling capacitor supplies the sudden current a chip draws when its logic switches (Microcontroller hardware). The current flows from the capacitor to the supply pin, through the chip, and back to the capacitor through the ground. Every millimetre of that path adds inductance, roughly 1 nH per millimetre of thin track, and inductance resists sudden changes of current:

ΔV=Ldidt50 nH of path and a 100 mA step in 10 ns: the supply at the pin dips by 0.5 V

Put the smallest capacitor nearest the pin, with short, wide tracks and the via right at the pad. Larger bulk capacitors can sit further away.

Controlled impedance

A fast signal sees a track as a transmission line with a characteristic impedance Z0, set by the track width, its height above the plane and the board material. If the impedance changes along the way, part of the signal reflects back, causing ringing. Radio needs 50 Ω tracks; USB 90 Ω and Ethernet 100 Ω between the two tracks of a differential pair.

For the 50 Ω track above, 0.33 mm wide, a signal travels about 167 mm per nanosecond; on an inner layer, about 145. A common rule of thumb: treat a track as a transmission line, with matched impedance and termination, once its delay is more than about a sixth of the signal's rise time. Fabs publish the widths for their stack-ups; use their numbers rather than the approximation, which gives 3.0 mm for a 1.6 mm board.

Parts and footprints

A footprint is the set of copper pads a part is soldered to, with its outline. Through-hole parts have leads through plated holes; surface-mount parts sit on pads on one face. Imperial size codes name small parts by length and width in hundredths of an inch: an 0805 is 0.08 × 0.05 inch, 2.0 × 1.25 mm.

Bringing up a new board

  1. Before soldering, look over the bare board and measure the resistance between each supply and ground: it must not be close to zero.
  2. After assembly, inspect fine-pitch pins for bridges under magnification, and measure each supply to ground again.
  3. Power it from a bench supply with the current limit set low, 50 to 100 mA. A short shows as the supply hitting its limit; switch off and find it.
  4. Measure every supply rail, then check the reset line, the clock and the programming connection.
  5. Load the smallest program that proves life: blink an LED, print over the UART.
  6. Bring up one part of the circuit at a time, and note what works. Watch for parts running hot.
