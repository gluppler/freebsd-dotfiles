The op-amp

An operational amplifier has two inputs and one output. It amplifies the difference between its inputs by an enormous gain A, 100 000 or more, so a few microvolts of difference drive the output all the way to one of its supply rails.

vout=A(v+−v−)

Used like this, with no feedback, it is a comparator. Every other use wraps feedback around it.

Negative feedback and the golden rules

Feed a fraction β of the output back and subtract it from the input. The amplifier then works on the error, and with A huge the error shrinks almost to nothing: the gain depends only on β, set by resistors, and no longer on the transistors inside.

yx=A1+Aβ≈1β

Two rules then solve almost every op-amp circuit:

  1. No current flows into either input.
  2. With negative feedback, the output does whatever makes v+=v−.

The inverting amplifier

vout=−RfRinvinZin=Rin

The input impedance is just Rin, since the far end of Rin sits at 0 V.

The non-inverting amplifier and the follower

vout=(1+RfRg)vin

Its input draws no current, so a non-inverting stage can read a weak source, a divider or a sensor without loading it. The follower, gain 1, does nothing else: it is a buffer.

Sums and differences

vout=−Rf(v1R1+v2R2+v3R3)
vout=R2R1(v2−v1)

With inputs from four logic pins through R, 2R, 4R and 8R, the summing amplifier is a 4-bit digital-to-analog converter. The difference amplifier measures current through a small shunt resistor that sits above ground; its accuracy depends on matched resistor pairs.

Integrators and differentiators

vout(t)=−1RC∫0tvindt

Swap R and C to differentiate. Real integrators drift on their own offset, so a large resistor across C limits the DC gain; real differentiators amplify noise, so a small resistor in series with C limits the high-frequency gain.

Active filters

A capacitor across Rf of an inverting amplifier makes a low-pass filter with gain, its output stiff for any load:

H(jω)=−RfRin11+jωRfCffc=12πRfCf

Two resistors and two capacitors around a follower give a second-order filter that falls 40 dB per decade, twice as steep:

fc=12πR1R2C1C2Q=R1R2C1C2C2(R1+R2)

With equal resistors Q=12C1/C2, so the maximally flat Butterworth response, Q=1/2, takes C1=2C2. A filter like this in front of an ADC removes frequencies above half the sampling rate, which would otherwise alias.

Comparators and the Schmitt trigger

Without feedback the output says which input is higher. Dedicated comparators such as the LM393 switch faster than op-amps and have open-collector outputs: add a pull-up to the logic supply.

Positive feedback makes the Schmitt trigger: once the output switches, it pulls the threshold the other way, so the input must travel back past a second threshold before it switches again.

VT=±R1R2Vsatnon-inverting, with the − input at 0 V

On a single supply, take the − input to a reference such as half the supply instead of ground. The 74HC14 is six Schmitt-trigger inverters, and most microcontroller inputs have a little hysteresis built in.

Real op-amps

Gain falls with frequency. Above a corner of a few hertz, the open-loop gain of most op-amps drops 20 dB per decade to 1 at the transition frequency fT, so gain times bandwidth is constant: an op-amp with a 1 MHz gain-bandwidth product gives 100 kHz at a gain of 10. Reading sensors

A photodiode gives a current proportional to light. A transimpedance amplifier holds the diode at 0 V and turns its current into a voltage:

vout=IpRf

A load cell or bridge sensor gives millivolts riding on half the supply. An instrumentation amplifier, three op-amps in one package, amplifies only the difference, with the gain set by one resistor RG:

G=1+49.4kΩRGAD620; the INA128 uses 50 kΩ
