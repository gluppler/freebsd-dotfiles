Driving and reading pins

A microcontroller pin set as an output is a push-pull stage: two transistors, one to the supply and one to ground, with only one on at a time. It can source current, out of the pin, or sink it, into the pin.

Each pin can carry only a few milliamps, typically 4 to 20 mA, with a total limit for the whole chip; check the datasheet. An LED from a pin needs a series resistor:

R=VDD−VFI3.3 V, a red LED at 2.0 V and 5 mA: 260 Ω

Anything heavier than an LED, such as a motor, relay or long LED strip, needs a transistor or a driver chip between it and the pin (Diodes and transistors).

An open-drain output has only the transistor to ground. It can pull a line low or let go, and a pull-up resistor brings the line high. Several open-drain outputs can share one line without harm: it is low if any of them pulls it low, which is how I2C works.

A floating input, connected to nothing, reads at random and can draw extra current. Give every input a defined level: an internal or external pull-up or pull-down, typically 10 to 100 kΩ.

Buttons and debouncing

A switch's contacts bounce for a millisecond or more as they close and open, so one press reads as many. Wire buttons from the pin to ground with a pull-up, so a press reads 0, and debounce in software: only accept a new level once it has been steady for several samples.

/* Call every millisecond with the pin's level; 1 is released, with a pull-up.
   The result changes only after five equal readings in a row. */
uint8_t debounce(uint8_t raw)
{
    static uint8_t state = 1, count;

    if (raw == state) {
        count = 0;
    } else if (++count >= 5) {
        state = raw;
        count = 0;
    }
    return state;
}

In hardware, an RC filter followed by a Schmitt-trigger input, such as a 74HC14, does the same. Choose RC longer than the bounce, around 10 ms.

UART

A UART sends bytes one bit at a time with no clock line: both ends agree the speed, the baud rate, in advance. The line idles high; a start bit pulls it low, eight data bits follow, least significant first, and a stop bit returns it high.

tbit=1baudbytes per second=baud10divisor=fclk16⋅baudwith 8 data bits, no parity and 1 stop bit, written 8N1

By the stop bit, a receiver running at the wrong speed has drifted by nine and a half bits' worth of error, and it must still land within the stop bit: the two ends must agree to within about 2% together. The RC oscillators inside many microcontrollers are not that accurate; use a crystal for reliable serial.

TX of one device goes to RX of the other, with grounds joined. Logic-level UART is not RS-232, which swings to ±12 V and is inverted, and must never be connected directly.

I2C

I2C connects many devices on two open-drain lines, SDA for data and SCL for the clock, each with a pull-up. Each device has a 7-bit address. The controller starts a transfer by pulling SDA low while SCL is high, sends the address and a read or write bit, and the addressed device pulls SDA low for one clock to acknowledge.

At 3.3 V in fast mode with 100 pF of bus: Rmin=2.9/0.003=967 Ω and Rmax=300ns/(0.8473×100pF)=3.54 kΩ, so 2.2 kΩ is a good choice. Breakout boards often carry their own pull-ups: several in parallel can fall below Rmin.

A device that stops answering mid-transfer can hold SDA low. Recover by clocking SCL nine times by hand until SDA is released, then sending a stop.

SPI

SPI uses a clock, SCLK, a line each way, MOSI and MISO, and a chip select per device, CS, active low. It is push-pull and has no addresses or acknowledgements, so it runs fast, tens of MHz. Every clock shifts one bit out and one bit in: a transfer is always an exchange.

The mode, bit order and maximum clock are in each device's datasheet. Pull CS high with a resistor so devices stay deselected while the microcontroller boots.

Regulators

A linear regulator drops the difference between input and output across a transistor, which turns it into heat. A low-dropout regulator, LDO, needs only a few hundred millivolts of headroom:

From 12 V to 3.3 V at 1 A, a linear regulator turns 8.7 W into heat and is 28% efficient. A switching regulator chops the input and smooths it with an inductor and a capacitor, at 85 to 95% efficiency.

A switcher's layout matters as much as its parts: keep the loop through the switch, diode and input capacitor as small as possible, as the module or chip datasheet shows. Use a linear regulator after a switcher to feed noise-sensitive analog circuits.

Batteries and sleep

A battery-powered device spends most of its time asleep. Its battery life depends on the average current, which a short burst of activity can dominate:

Iavg=DIactive+(1−D)Isleept=CIavgD is the fraction of time awake; C the capacity in mAh

A CR2032 coin cell holds about 225 mAh, so it lasts 225/0.205≈1098 hours, 46 days. Waking every 10 s instead gives 375 days. Coin cells also cannot deliver large pulses: add a capacitor across them for radio bursts.

Decoupling and clocks

Every chip draws its supply current in short spikes as its logic switches. A capacitor beside each supply pin supplies the spikes locally, so they do not travel along the supply track. Real capacitors have inductance in their leads and pads, so each works only up to its resonant frequency:

|Z|=ESR2+(2πfL−12πfC)2f0=12πLC

A crystal needs two load capacitors to oscillate at its marked frequency. The crystal's datasheet gives its load capacitance CL; the pins and tracks add a stray few picofarads:

C1=C2=2(CL−Cstray)CL=12.5 pF with 4 pF stray: 17 pF, so fit 18 pF

Protecting inputs

Inputs connected to the outside world meet static, wrong voltages and reversed power. Each pin has clamp diodes to its supply and ground, which survive only a few milliamps; a series resistor limits the current into them:

Reading sensors

Many sensors change resistance, read through a voltage divider into an ADC (DC circuits and their laws). A thermistor's resistance falls steeply and non-linearly with temperature; the beta equation converts it back:

1T=1T0+1βlnRR0temperatures in kelvin; for a common part R0=10 kΩ at T0=298.15 K and β=3950 K

An ADC input charges a small sampling capacitor each conversion. A source impedance above about 10 kΩ cannot charge it in time and reads low: add a 100 nF capacitor from the ADC pin to ground, or a slower sampling time. Measure a battery through a divider of large resistors, with that capacitor, so it drains only microamps.
