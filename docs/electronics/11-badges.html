What a badge is made of

A badge is a board worn on a lanyard at a conference or festival: a name tag, an artwork and a small computer in one. Most share a design: a microcontroller with Wi-Fi or a radio, LEDs, buttons, often a display, a battery with its charger, and connectors for add-ons.

Power sets most of a badge's design (Batteries and sleep): a CR2032 coin cell lasts days with a few LEDs and deep sleep, two AAA cells give more current, and a LiPo cell with a charger runs displays and radios for a day.

Board art

Badges use the board itself as the artwork. Each layer is a colour to paint with: copper under the mask shows a lighter shade of the mask colour, bare board under it a darker one; exposed copper is plated gold with ENIG; silkscreen prints on top; and bare FR-4, with neither copper nor mask, lets light through.

Draw the art in a vector program, import it as copper, mask or silkscreen layers in the PCB program, and keep artwork copper away from the circuit's nets. Reverse-mount LEDs, soldered on the back and shining through a hole, light the board from behind. Mask comes in green, black, white, red, blue, yellow and purple; choose ENIG for any exposed copper art, since it stays flat and does not tarnish.

Simple add-ons

The SAO connector, born on DEF CON badges, lets anyone build a small board that plugs into any badge that has one. Version 2 has six pins: 3.3 V power, ground, an I2C bus and two GPIO pins. The Communicator's schematic labels it Simple_Addon_v2.

An add-on with an I2C chip must use an address no other device on the badge uses. Keep it light and low power: it hangs on the header and runs from the badge's battery.

Tildagon hexpansions

The Tildagon has six hexpansion ports, edge connectors that take hexagonal add-on boards. Each port provides 3.3 V at up to 600 mA, its own I2C bus, four fast GPIO pins wired to the ESP32-S3 and five slower ones on a GPIO expander that can also drive LEDs.

A hexpansion with an I2C EEPROM, at address 0x50 or 0x57, can identify itself and even carry its own app. The EEPROM starts with a 32-byte header; this builds one, and is checked against the example in the documentation:

def header(vid, pid, name, unique=0, offset=32, page=32, size=8192):
    """A hexpansion EEPROM header: 32 bytes, little-endian, the last one a checksum."""
    data = b'THEX2024' + struct.pack('<HHIHHH', offset, page, size, vid, pid, unique)
    data += name.encode('ascii')[:9].ljust(9, b'\0')
    checksum = 0x55
    for byte in data[1:]:
        checksum ^= byte
    return data + bytes([checksum])

Get a vendor and product ID before shipping one: the documentation explains the shared IDs and how to flash the EEPROM from a computer with mpremote.

Charlieplexing LEDs

A pin can be high, low, or an input that floats, connected to nothing. Charlieplexing uses all three: with one pin high, one low and the rest floating, exactly one LED between the high and the low pin lights. Stepping through every pair faster than the eye can follow seems to light any pattern.

N=n(n−1)n pins drive N LEDs: 3 pins 6 LEDs, 5 pins 20, 10 pins 90

Each LED is lit for only 1/N of the time, so it looks dimmer: drive it harder, within the pin's limits, or use fewer LEDs per group. Keep the supply below twice the LEDs' forward voltage so the series paths stay dark.

Addressable LEDs

Addressable LEDs, such as the WS2812B, contain a red, green and blue LED and a small controller. They chain on one data line: each keeps the first colour it receives and passes the rest on. The data is a pulse per bit, its width telling 0 from 1.

Imax≈N×60mAtframe=30μs×N+tresetfull white draws about 20 mA per colour; each LED takes 24 bits of 1.25 µs

Sixty LEDs at full white draw 3.6 A from 5 V, so budget power first and limit brightness in software. Their data input expects at least 0.7 × 5 V = 3.5 V for a 1, which a 3.3 V microcontroller does not reliably give: add a 74AHCT125 buffer (Level shifting). Put a large capacitor across the supply where the power enters, and a 100 nF capacitor at each LED.

Key matrices

Keyboards wire their keys in a grid of rows and columns, so many keys need few pins. The microcontroller drives one row low at a time and reads the columns, which have pull-ups: a pressed key in that row pulls its column low.

keys=r×cpins=r+c60 keys in 5 rows of 12 columns need 17 pins

The diodes also let any number of keys be pressed at once, which games and fast typists need. Debounce every key as in Buttons and debouncing. The Communicator hands its keyboard to a TCA8418, a chip that scans the matrix and reports keys over I2C.

/* Rows are outputs, columns inputs with pull-ups; each key's diode
   points from its column to its row. */
void scan(uint8_t pressed[ROWS][COLS])
{
    for (int r = 0; r < ROWS; r++) {
        row_low(r);                     /* the other rows float */
        delay_us(5);                    /* let the lines settle */
        for (int c = 0; c < COLS; c++)
            pressed[r][c] = !column_read(c);
        row_float(r);
    }
}

Touch pads

A copper pad under the solder mask makes a touch button with no moving parts. The pad has a few picofarads of capacitance to its surroundings, and a finger adds a few more (Capacitors). Charged through a resistor, the pad takes longer to reach the input threshold when touched:

t=RClnVDDVDD−Vth1 MΩ: 6.9 µs alone and 10.4 µs with a 5 pF finger

Many microcontrollers, the ESP32-S3 among them, have touch inputs that do this in hardware. Make pads about the size of a fingertip, 10 to 15 mm, keep a gap to the ground pour around them, and run their tracks short and away from noisy signals.

Charging a lithium cell

A lithium cell charges at a constant current until it reaches 4.2 V, then at a constant 4.2 V while the current falls away. The charger stops when the current drops to a tenth or so of the starting value.

Icharge=1000VRPROGthe resistor on its PROG pin sets the current: 2 kΩ for 500 mA

Charge small LiPo cells at 0.5C to 1C, never below freezing, and always through a charger IC. Use protected cells, or add a protection chip, so a short or a flat battery cannot damage the cell.
