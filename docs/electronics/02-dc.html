Charge, current, voltage and power

Charge q is measured in coulombs; an electron carries 1.602×10−19 C. Current is the rate at which charge passes a point, so one ampere is one coulomb each second:

i=dqdt

Voltage is the energy each unit of charge gains or loses between two points, one joule per coulomb being one volt. A source raises charge to a higher potential; a resistor lets it fall and gives the energy off as heat.

v=dwdq

Power, the rate of energy transfer, is their product, and energy is power over time:

p=viw=∫pdt

A battery's capacity is given as charge, in milliampere-hours. Multiplying by its voltage gives energy: a 2000 mAh cell at 3.7 V holds 7.4 Wh. At a steady 50 mA it lasts about 40 hours.

t≈QIavgE=QV

Ohm's law

In a resistor the current is proportional to the voltage across it. The constant is the resistance; its inverse is the conductance G, in siemens.

v=iRG=1R

All the power a resistor takes becomes heat, and its rating, often 1/8 or 1/4 W for small parts, must exceed it. 5 V across 220 Ω drives 22.7 mA and dissipates 114 mW.

P=VI=I2R=V2R

A conductor's resistance grows with its length L and shrinks with its cross-section A; copper's resistivity ρ is 1.68×10−8 Ω·m, which matters for long wires and thin board traces.

R=ρLA

Kirchhoff's current law

Charge is conserved and cannot pile up in a wire, so whatever flows into a node flows out. Counting currents into the node as positive and out as negative, they sum to zero.

∑kik=0

Kirchhoff's voltage law

Potential is like height. Walk any closed loop and you come back to where you started, so the rises and drops met on the way sum to zero.

∑kvk=0

The wires are flat on the graph: an ideal wire has no resistance, so no voltage across it, and every point of one node sits at one potential.

Series and parallel

Resistances in series add. In parallel their conductances add, so the result is always smaller than the smallest resistor.

Rs=R1+R2+⋯+Rn
1Rp=1R1+1R2+⋯+1RnR1∥R2=R1R2R1+R2

Two equal resistors in parallel make half the value; 10 kΩ in parallel with 1 kΩ is 909 Ω, close to the smaller one.

Voltage and current dividers

Two resistors in series across a voltage share it in proportion to their resistance. This is the most common circuit there is: it scales a voltage down for an ADC, sets a bias point or a regulator's output, and is what a potentiometer is.

Vout=VinR2R1+R2

The formula assumes nothing draws current from the output. A load RL sits in parallel with R2 and pulls the output down; keep the load at least ten times R2, or buffer it. To read a lithium cell, up to 4.2 V, with a 3.3 V ADC, two 100 kΩ resistors halve it to 2.1 V.

Current divides between parallel paths in inverse proportion to their resistance; more of it takes the easier path.

I1=IR2R1+R2I2=IR1R1+R2

Thévenin and Norton equivalents

Seen from two terminals, any network of resistors and sources behaves exactly like one voltage source in series with one resistor (Thévenin), or one current source in parallel with one resistor (Norton). To find them:

  1. Measure or compute the open-circuit voltage at the terminals: that is Vth.
  2. Zero every independent source, a voltage source becoming a wire and a current source a gap, and find the resistance looking into the terminals: that is Rth. Or short the terminals, find the current Isc, and divide.
  3. The Norton source is the short-circuit current, with the same resistance in parallel.
Vth=VocRth=VocIscIN=Isc=VthRth

Superposition

In a linear circuit, one with only resistors, linear dependent sources and independent sources, the response to several sources is the sum of the responses to each alone, with the others zeroed.

Superposition holds for voltages and currents but not for power, which is a product of the two.

Node analysis: the general method

Dividers and equivalents solve small circuits; node analysis solves any of them, and is what circuit simulators do.

  1. Choose one node as ground.
  2. Name the voltage of every other node: v1,v2,…
  3. At each of those nodes, write Kirchhoff's current law, with every resistor current written by Ohm's law as (va−vb)/R.
  4. Solve the linear equations, G𝐯=𝐢.

The current leaving node 1 through its resistors equals the source current, and at node 2 the currents balance:

v1R1+v1−v2R2=ISv2−v1R2+v2R3=0

In matrix form each resistor appears as its conductance:

[1R1+1R2−1R2−1R21R2+1R3][v1v2]=[IS0]

The algorithm

A program builds G by stamping each part into it: a resistor between nodes a and b adds its conductance to two diagonal entries and subtracts it from the two entries that join them. Gaussian elimination then solves the system.

def solve(A, b):
    """Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting."""
    n = len(b)
    M = [row[:] + [b[r]] for r, row in enumerate(A)]
    for c in range(n):
        top = max(range(c, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][c]))
        M[c], M[top] = M[top], M[c]
        for r in range(c + 1, n):
            k = M[r][c] / M[c][c]
            for j in range(c, n + 1):
                M[r][j] -= k * M[c][j]
    x = [0.0] * n
    for r in reversed(range(n)):
        x[r] = (M[r][n] - sum(M[r][j] * x[j] for j in range(r + 1, n))) / M[r][r]
    return x


def nodal(nodes, resistors, sources):
    """Node voltages. Resistors are (a, b, ohms); current sources are
    (a, b, amps) pushing current from node a to node b. Node 0 is ground."""
    G = [[0.0] * nodes for _ in range(nodes)]
    i = [0.0] * nodes
    for a, b, ohms in resistors:
        g = 1 / ohms
        for n in (a, b):
            if n:
                G[n - 1][n - 1] += g
        if a and b:
            G[a - 1][b - 1] -= g
            G[b - 1][a - 1] -= g
    for a, b, amps in sources:
        if a:
            i[a - 1] -= amps
        if b:
            i[b - 1] += amps
    return solve(G, i)


print(nodal(2, [(1, 0, 1e3), (1, 2, 1e3), (2, 0, 2e3)], [(0, 1, 1e-3)]))

Run, it prints [0.75, 0.5]. Voltage sources need one extra unknown each, their current, which gives modified node analysis: the form inside SPICE.

Maximum power transfer

A source with internal resistance Rth delivers the most power to a load of equal resistance. Half the power is then lost inside the source, so this matters for weak signals, antennas and audio, not for batteries or supplies, which should feed loads far larger than their own resistance.

PL=Vth2RL(Rth+RL)2Pmax=Vth24Rth

Real sources

V=Voc−IRint

Measure Voc with no load, then the voltage V at a known current: Rint=(Voc−V)/I. Coin cells have high internal resistance, so a radio burst of tens of milliamperes sags their voltage; a capacitor across the cell supplies the burst instead.
