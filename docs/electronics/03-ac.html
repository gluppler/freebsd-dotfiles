Capacitors

A capacitor stores charge on two plates separated by an insulator, the dielectric. The charge is proportional to the voltage, so changing the voltage takes current, and a fast change takes a lot.

q=Cvi=CdvdtW=12Cv2
C=ε0εrAdplate area A, gap d, ε0=8.85pF/m

Its voltage cannot jump, since that would need infinite current: a capacitor holds a voltage steady, which is why every chip has one beside its supply pins. Capacitors in parallel add; in series they combine like resistors in parallel.

Cpar=C1+C21Cser=1C1+1C2

Charging and the time constant

Inductors

An inductor stores energy in the magnetic field of its current, and resists any change in that current by producing a voltage. Its current cannot jump, as a capacitor's voltage cannot.

v=LdidtW=12Li2
i(t)=VR(1−e−t/τ)τ=LR

Without D1, the current would stop in nanoseconds, and v=Ldi/dt would put hundreds of volts across the transistor. Every relay, solenoid and motor switched by a transistor needs this flyback diode, cathode to the supply.

Two coils on one core make a transformer, which trades voltage for current in the ratio of their turns and transforms impedance by its square:

Sine waves

Any steady AC signal is built from sine waves, and linear circuits keep their shape, changing only size and timing. A sine has a peak Vp, a frequency f and a phase φ:

v(t)=Vpsin(ωt+φ)ω=2πf=2πT

The root-mean-square value is the DC voltage that would heat a resistor equally. Mains of 230 V RMS peaks at 325 V.

Vrms=1T∫0Tv2dt=Vp2Vpp=2Vp

Phasors and impedance

Euler's formula turns the rotating arrow into a complex number. Each sine of one frequency becomes a phasor V=Vpejφ, and j marks the imaginary part, since i is taken by current.

ejθ=cosθ+jsinθv(t)=Re{Vejωt}

Differentiating becomes multiplying by jω, so capacitors and inductors obey Ohm's law with a complex resistance, the impedance Z:

V=IZZR=RZC=1jωCZL=jωL

Series and parallel rules, dividers, node analysis and Thévenin all work unchanged with impedances, calculated in complex numbers.

RC filters and Bode plots

An RC divider whose lower arm is a capacitor passes slow signals and shorts fast ones to ground: a low-pass filter. Its transfer function is the divider formula with impedances.

H(jω)=voutvin=11+jωRCfc=12πRC
|H|=11+(f/fc)2φ=−arctanffc

Swap R and C and the filter passes high frequencies instead:

H(jω)=jωRC1+jωRC

Decibels compare two levels on a log scale; a voltage ratio uses 20 log, a power ratio 10 log, so both give the same number. −3 dB is a voltage ratio of 0.707, −20 dB of 0.1.

dB=20log10V2V1=10log10P2P1

Second-order circuits and resonance

A capacitor and an inductor together pass energy back and forth: the capacitor's charge drives a current through the inductor, whose current then recharges the capacitor the other way.

ω0=1LCf0=12πLC

Resistance takes energy out on every swing. A series RLC circuit is described by its natural frequency and its damping ratio ζ, or the quality factor Q:

ζ=R2CLQ=12ζ=1RLC

Driven by a sine, a series RLC circuit's reactances cancel at ω0, leaving only R: the current peaks there. The higher the Q, the narrower the peak; its width between the −3 dB points is f0/Q. Radios tune this way.

Power in AC circuits

With voltage and current out of phase, part of the energy only sloshes back and forth. The average, real power counts just the in-phase part; the rest is reactive power, and their combination is the apparent power a supply must carry.

P=VrmsIrmscosφQ=VrmsIrmssinφS=VrmsIrms

The power factor cosφ is 1 for a resistor and 0 for an ideal capacitor or inductor. Units: watts for P, volt-amperes reactive for Q, volt-amperes for S.
